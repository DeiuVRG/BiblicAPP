Having read the short works that I recently composed for you, my dearest son Marcellinus, on the baptism of infants and on the perfection of human righteousness, to the effect that no one in this life seems either to have attained it or to be going to attain it, except the one Mediator, who endured human things in the likeness of the flesh of sin, yet without any sin at all, you wrote back that you are troubled by my saying in the latter of the two books that it can happen that a human being be without sin, if his will does not fail and divine help assists it, and yet that, except for the one in whom all will be made alive, no one has been, or will be, in whom that perfection would be found while he lives here. For it seems absurd to you to say that something can happen for which an example is lacking, when, as I believe, you do not doubt that it has never happened that a camel passed through the eye of a needle, and yet he said that this too is possible for God; you may read, too, that twelve thousand legions of angels could have fought for Christ to keep him from suffering, and yet it did not happen; you may read that it could have happened that the nations were exterminated all at once from the land that was being given to the children of Israel, and yet God willed that it happen little by little; and six hundred other things may come to mind that we admit could have happened or could happen, and yet we cannot produce examples of their having happened. Hence we ought not to deny that it is possible for a human being to be without sin merely because there is no human being, apart from the one who is not only man but also God by nature, in whom we can show that this has been perfectly achieved.
